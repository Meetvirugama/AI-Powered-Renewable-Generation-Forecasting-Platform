1.  Executive Summary
Solar and wind generation is decided by the weather, not by the operator. Output moves with cloud cover, wind speed, temperature, time of day and season, and it does so on a timescale much shorter than the planning decisions that depend on it. A grid operator has to commit to a schedule before knowing what the resource will actually deliver, and every megawatt of mismatch between that commitment and reality has to be absorbed somewhere — by backup generation, by storage, by curtailment, or by a settlement charge.
The problem statement asks for a platform that ingests weather data, historical generation records and site-level parameters, forecasts solar and wind output over the next 24 to 72 hours, flags periods of expected over- and under-generation, and recommends grid actions such as curtailment, storage dispatch and backup activation. Our reading of that requirement is that the forecast is the input to the deliverable, not the deliverable itself. The deliverable is the decision.
We therefore propose a platform built around a single end-to-end chain: forecast, quantify the uncertainty in that forecast, detect where the forecast disagrees with the declared schedule, convert that disagreement into a rupee figure under the applicable deviation settlement rules, optimise the schedule and storage action that minimises that figure, and explain the result with both statistical attribution and the regulatory clause it rests on.
WHAT DISTINGUISHES THIS SUBMISSION
We produce a distribution, not a number. Each 15-minute block is forecast as P10, P50 and P90 rather than a single expected value, because an operator planning reserve needs to know how wrong the forecast could plausibly be.
We price the uncertainty. Forecast spread is carried forward into a deterministic deviation-settlement engine so that risk is expressed in rupees, not in megawatt-error.
We optimise the action, not just report the forecast. A mixed-integer optimiser selects the schedule and battery dispatch with the lowest expected settlement cost across the forecast distribution.
We keep the language model out of the arithmetic. Every generation, deviation, cost and optimisation value is computed by deterministic modules; retrieval and generation are confined to explanation.
2.  Problem Understanding
2.1  The requirement as stated
Reduced to its essentials, the problem statement asks for five capabilities. The platform must ingest weather data, historical generation records and site-level parameters. It must forecast solar and wind generation across a 24 to 72 hour horizon. It must identify periods of expected over-generation and under-generation. It must recommend grid actions, with curtailment, storage dispatch and backup activation given as examples. And it must serve four distinct users — grid operators, utility companies, renewable plant owners and energy traders — whose questions differ even when the underlying forecast is the same.
The suggested technologies — time-series forecasting libraries, weather and satellite APIs, real-time dashboards and cloud data pipelines — are offered as options rather than constraints. We treat them that way: we adopt the architecture they imply, and we substitute specific models where we can justify the substitution empirically.
2.2  The structural difficulty beneath the requirement
The difficulty is not that renewable generation is hard to predict in the abstract. It is that the consequences of a prediction error are asymmetric, time-resolved and financially settled.
Dimension of difficulty
Why it complicates the problem
Commitment precedes information
Schedules must be declared before the weather is known. The forecast is not an observation of the future; it is a commitment made under uncertainty.
Errors are settled, not absorbed
In the Indian context, mismatch between scheduled and actual injection is handled through a deviation settlement mechanism. A forecast error is therefore a financial event, not merely a technical one.
Consequences are asymmetric
Over-generation and under-generation call for entirely different responses — charging or curtailment in one case, discharge or reserve in the other — and they do not carry symmetric costs.
Resolution is fine and horizon is long
Operational scheduling works at 15-minute resolution, so a 72-hour horizon is 288 separate decisions, each with its own uncertainty.
Uncertainty is heteroscedastic
Forecast error is not constant. It grows with horizon, spikes in unsettled weather, and behaves differently for solar ramps than for wind lulls. A single average error figure hides exactly the periods that matter.
Users ask different questions
An operator asks what reserve to hold. A plant owner asks whether the asset is underperforming. A trader asks what the exposure is worth. One forecast has to support all three.
3.  The Gap We Identified
Most renewable forecasting work — including most hackathon responses to problems of this shape — terminates at a point forecast. A model is trained, an error metric is reported, a chart is drawn, and the output is a line on a graph. That line is genuinely useful, but it does not answer the question the user actually has to act on.
What the operator has to decide
What a point forecast tells them
How much reserve should I hold tomorrow evening?
Nothing. A single expected value contains no information about downside risk.
Which of several defensible schedules should I declare?
Nothing. Every schedule near the forecast looks equally reasonable.
What will it cost me if this forecast is wrong?
Nothing. Megawatt error is not the unit the decision is made in.
Should I charge the battery now or hold capacity for later?
Nothing directly. The trade-off depends on the spread of outcomes, not the mean.
Can I justify this decision to a regulator or a board?
Partially. The number exists, but not the reasoning or the rule behind it.
OUR DESIGN INSIGHT
The distance between a good forecast and a good decision is uncertainty, priced. A platform that reports P50 alone has done the modelling work and stopped one step short of the value. Our system treats the forecast distribution as the starting point of the product rather than its conclusion.
4.  Proposed Solution
4.1  Solution statement
We propose an AI-powered renewable generation forecasting and decision-support platform that combines weather forecasts, historical generation data, physics-based generation modelling, machine learning, probabilistic forecasting and mathematical optimisation.
The platform collects weather forecasts, historical generation observations and plant and site parameters. A data-quality and feature-engineering layer converts these into model-ready features. A forecasting layer combines a physical generation model with machine-learning models — LightGBM under a quantile objective and the Chronos-2 time-series foundation model — while retaining a persistence baseline as the reference against which any claim of skill must be measured.
Instead of a single point forecast, the system produces a calibrated probabilistic forecast represented by multiple quantiles, reported operationally as P10, P50 and P90 for each 15-minute block. This distribution is passed to a decision engine that compares it against the declared schedule, computes the expected deviation exposure under the applicable settlement rules, and searches for the schedule and storage dispatch that minimise that exposure. Curtailment advisories, reserve requirements and portfolio pooling scenarios are evaluated in the same framework.
Results are presented through an interactive dashboard showing forecast bands, risk, recommended actions and expected financial impact. A retrieval-augmented regulatory assistant explains the regulatory basis of the computed result using the relevant CERC and IEGC material.
4.2  Design principles
Principle
How it shapes the build
Decision-first
Every component is justified by a decision it improves. Features that do not change an action are classified as secondary.
Uncertainty is first-class
Quantiles are produced by the model itself, not derived post hoc from a residual assumption, and they propagate through every downstream layer.
Money is computed, never generated
All deviation, cost and optimisation values come from deterministic code. The language model never produces a number.
Physics before parameters
A physical estimate of expected generation is computed first and supplied to the learner as a feature, so the model corrects a grounded prior rather than learning the sun from scratch.
Honest claims only
Anything not implemented is labelled as design or future work. Data limitations are stated in the report rather than discovered in the demo.

Responsibility
1 · Data ingestion

ARCHITECTURAL BOUNDARY
The boundary between layers 4 and 6 is deliberate and is the most important design decision in the system. Everything numerical happens in layer 4 through deterministic computation. Layer 6 may retrieve, summarise and explain, but it never originates a value. This removes the most common failure mode in LLM-assisted analytics, which is a confidently stated figure that no calculation produced.
6.  Forecasting Methodology
The forecasting engine is physics-informed rather than purely statistical. A deterministic physical model supplies an estimate of expected generation from the weather forecast and site parameters; the machine-learning layer then learns the correction between that estimate and observed output. This matters because the physical model already encodes the parts of the relationship that are not worth learning from data — solar geometry, clear-sky irradiance, the shape of a turbine power curve — leaving the learner free to capture soiling, shading, local micro-climate and systematic instrument bias.

6.2  Why these models
The problem statement suggests Prophet, LSTM and XGBoost. These are listed as technologies that may be used rather than as requirements, and our selection differs on deliberate grounds. LightGBM is chosen because it supports a quantile objective directly, which means the probabilistic output is produced by the model rather than assumed afterwards, and because gradient-boosted trees handle the tabular, feature-rich structure of this problem efficiently. Chronos-2 is included because a pretrained time-series foundation model gives a strong result with limited site-specific history, which is the realistic condition for a newly commissioned plant. Persistence is retained not because it is competitive but because it defines the floor: a model that cannot beat persistence has demonstrated nothing.
The selection between these is empirical. Rolling-origin backtesting determines the champion per site and per horizon band, and the report of that comparison is part of the deliverable rather than a detail hidden inside the code.

8.  Operational Workflow
The platform runs as a scheduled cycle rather than as an on-demand tool, because scheduling decisions are themselves periodic. Figure 4 shows the full cycle including the branches that matter most in practice: what the system does when input data is degraded, and what it does on a day when no action is warranted.
Figure 4. Activity Diagram — Daily Forecast-to-Action Operating Cycle
The two branch paths on the right are deliberate. A system that only describes its happy path has not been designed for operation.
8.1  Notes on the branches
Degraded input data. If quality checks fail, the system does not silently produce a confident forecast from bad inputs. It falls back to persistence and marks the output as reduced-confidence, so the operator knows the difference between a forecast and a guess.
No-action days. If the forecast band and the declared schedule agree within tolerance, the schedule is published unchanged and logged. Recommending an intervention every cycle would train users to ignore the system.
Parallel evaluation. Deviation exposure and storage availability are independent computations and are evaluated concurrently before the optimiser runs.
Explanation last. The explanation layer is invoked only after the numbers exist. It describes a decision that has already been computed; it does not participate in making it.
9.  Decision Intelligence and Economics
This section describes the step that separates the platform from a forecasting notebook. Figure 5 traces a single operating cycle from the probabilistic forecast through to the emitted action set.
Figure 5. From Forecast Uncertainty to a Priced, Optimised Grid Action
The optimisation panel is illustrative of the mechanism. Actual values are computed per site and per block at run time.
9.1  Over- and under-generation
Over-generation is the condition where expected generation exceeds the declared schedule. It creates surplus energy and calls for battery charging, schedule revision, or — where neither is available — curtailment. Under-generation is the reverse: expected generation falls short of schedule, creating a deviation that calls for battery discharge, reserve availability or backup consideration. The platform detects both against the forecast band rather than against the central forecast alone, so a block where the schedule sits inside the band is treated differently from one where it sits outside it.
9.2  Deviation settlement as the unit of risk
Because the problem is positioned within the Indian electricity ecosystem, deviation from schedule is not merely an operational inconvenience — it is settled under the deviation settlement mechanism framework maintained by the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission, alongside the specific framework for forecasting, scheduling and imbalance handling of renewable generation. Our system implements this as a deterministic computation layer: block-wise deviation, the applicable charge under the notified structure, and the probability-weighted expectation across the forecast distribution.
A WORDING PRECISION WE MAINTAIN THROUGHOUT
The deviation settlement engine estimates the financial consequence of departing from the scheduled generation under the applicable regulatory rules. It is not an electricity price model, and we do not describe it as one.
Similarly, the platform identifies periods where reserve or backup capacity may be required and flags them. It does not activate backup generation. The system is decision support, not autonomous grid control, and the distinction is maintained in the interface as well as in this document.
9.3  Optimisation
With deviation priced, schedule selection becomes an optimisation problem rather than a judgement call. The optimiser evaluates candidate schedules against the full forecast distribution and returns the one with the lowest expected settlement cost, jointly with a battery dispatch profile subject to state-of-charge, power and energy constraints. The reported benefit is the difference between the expected cost of the naive central-forecast schedule and the expected cost of the optimised schedule — a quantity the user can audit rather than take on trust.
9.4  Portfolio pooling
Aggregating multiple plants at a pooling level reduces net forecast error, because independent errors across sites partially cancel. This is recognised in the regulatory treatment of aggregated wind and solar projects, and it is a genuine operational lever rather than a hypothetical one. The platform therefore supports portfolio-aware forecasting and scheduling: pooled forecast, pooled deviation, and the resulting change in expected exposure compared with settling each plant individually.

11.2  Deployment architecture
Figure 6. Cloud Deployment Architecture
The React build is served as static assets from S3 through CloudFront. Application traffic reaches containerised FastAPI and worker services on EC2 through an application load balancer. Forecasts, schedules, computed costs and the regulatory vector index live in RDS; model artefacts, raw source pulls and versioned forecast archives live in S3. CloudWatch provides logging and alarms, and configuration and credentials are held in SSM Parameter Store rather than in the container image.

VALIDATION DISCIPLINE
All validation uses rolling-origin, time-ordered splits. No future information enters a training fold, and horizon-wise errors are reported separately because a 6-hour forecast and a 66-hour forecast are different problems.
Where the physical simulation of demonstration-plant generation is used, it is driven by real weather data and is validated against the available real Indian inverter-generation record. We do not present simulated generation as long-term real operational data.

15.  Scope, Assumptions and Known Limitations
We state these explicitly because a system that is honest about its boundaries is more useful than one that is not, and because every limitation below has a defined path forward.
Item
Statement
Weather data resolution
The forecasting engine operates on 15-minute generation intervals. Native 15-minute weather data is not available for every variable in every region; where it is unavailable for the target region, hourly forecasts are explicitly interpolated or resampled to the modelling interval and the assumption is recorded with the forecast.
Validation dataset size
The current prototype is validated against a limited real Indian generation record. We do not claim multi-season validation from it. Broader multi-season and multi-site validation is planned and is stated as future work rather than implied as complete.
Simulated generation
Demonstration-plant generation is physics-simulated from real weather and validated against the available real inverter-generation data. It is not presented as long-term real operational plant data.
Backup activation
The platform flags periods where reserve or backup capacity may be required. It does not control generation assets. Any claim of automated backup activation would be an overclaim and is not made.
Net-load forecasting
Net load requires demand data that the problem statement does not supply. The architecture supports the extension; the capability is not claimed as present.
Marginal emissions
True marginal grid emissions require grid-mix and marginal-emission data. We report potential fossil-generation displacement instead, which is defensible from the data we hold.
Trading functionality
Without real-time market price feeds, the trader-facing capability is imbalance exposure estimation rather than a trading engine.
Digital twin terminology
We describe a physics-based site performance model and a digital-twin-inspired monitoring layer. We do not model equipment states, sensor states or plant topology, so we do not use the unqualified term.
Regulatory currency
Deviation settlement regulation is amended periodically. The settlement logic is implemented as a configurable rule layer so that amendments can be applied without retraining or redeploying the forecasting models.

17.  Conclusion
The problem statement asks for a platform that forecasts renewable generation and recommends grid actions. Our interpretation is that the second half of that sentence is where the value sits, and that it can only be served properly if the first half produces a distribution rather than a number.
Our architecture therefore reads as a single chain: forecast, quantify uncertainty, detect over- and under-generation, price the resulting deviation risk, optimise the schedule and storage response, recommend a concrete action, and explain both the statistics and the regulation behind it. Every feature in the system attaches to one of those links. Nothing in it exists because it was technically interesting.
We have also been deliberate about what we do not claim. The system recommends rather than actuates. It reports potential fossil displacement rather than marginal emissions. It estimates imbalance exposure rather than trading outcomes. Its validation dataset is limited and we say so. We believe a solution that is precise about its boundaries is more credible than one that is expansive about its capabilities, and that this precision is itself part of what makes the design fit for an environment where decisions are financially settled and regulatorily reviewed.
The proposed platform does not stop at predicting renewable generation. It propagates forecast uncertainty into operational and financial decision-making to recommend the action that minimises expected grid imbalance and associated cost.
